\chapter{Conclusions} \label{chapConclusions}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This thesis has studied the design, modeling, optimization, and experimental validation of bio-inspired aquatic robots. The central theme of the thesis is that aquatic locomotion should not be treated as the output of a controller acting on a fixed mechanical system. Instead, swimming emerges from the coupled interaction among morphology, compliance, actuation, hydrodynamic loading, sensing, and control. From this perspective, the thesis has approached bio-inspired locomotion as a problem of embodied multiphysics, where physical design and dynamical modeling are developed together in order to improve robotic swimming performance.

The main contributions of the thesis are organized around four connected directions. First, the thesis introduced a programmable online stiffness modulation framework for aquatic propulsion, in which joint stiffness is treated as a time-varying control input rather than as a fixed mechanical parameter. Second, it developed variational and differentiable models that support optimization of swimming motion, stiffness schedules, and strongly coupled fluid--robot interaction. Third, it presented physical robotic platforms for validating these ideas in real water, including both fixed-base and untethered swimming experiments. Finally, it discussed sensing as an enabling component for future closed-loop aquatic robots, connecting local state estimation and flow interaction to the broader goal of autonomous bio-inspired swimming.

\section{Programmable Stiffness for Optimized Aquatic Propulsion}

The central technical contribution of this thesis is the programmable online stiffness modulation (POSM) framework. In most robotic swimmers, stiffness is either fixed by material selection and geometry or adjusted only at a low bandwidth. In contrast, POSM converts stiffness into a within-cycle control variable. The proposed mechanism uses sliding-laminate flexures to realize time-varying, joint-specific compliance through a compact actuation architecture. This allows different joints along a swimming appendage to follow different stiffness schedules during a single stroke.

To make this mechanism useful for locomotion design, the thesis developed a variational-mechanics model of the multi-joint appendage with quasi-steady hydrodynamic loading. This model enabled trajectory optimization over both appendage motion and joint stiffness. As a result, stiffness schedules could be designed directly for propulsive objectives such as thrust generation, swimming speed, and cost of transport, rather than chosen through manual tuning.

Hardware experiments demonstrated the value of this approach. In fixed-base tests, programmable joint-specific stiffness improved thrust generation compared with static and spatially uniform stiffness baselines. In untethered experiments, the same design principle was validated on a fish-inspired robot capable of both body--caudal-fin (BCF) undulation and drag-based pectoral-fin paddling. Across these tests, POSM achieved up to 89\% higher thrust in drag-based swimming, up to 19\% higher mean thrust in BCF swimming, and up to 34.8\% reduction in cost of transport. These results show that variable stiffness can be more than a passive design feature: when integrated with modeling and optimization, it becomes an active mechanism for shaping fluid--robot interaction.

\section{Variational Modeling and Fluid--Robot Optimization}

A second major contribution of the thesis is the use of variational modeling as a unifying framework for aquatic robot dynamics. Bio-inspired swimming involves multibody motion, elastic deformation, actuation constraints, and hydrodynamic loading. These ingredients are difficult to combine in a way that is both physically meaningful and useful for optimization. By formulating the robot dynamics through variational principles and discretizing them with structure-preserving methods, the thesis developed models that are stable, differentiable, and suitable for trajectory optimization.

For the POSM system, this modeling approach provided a reduced-order but optimization-ready description of the swimming appendage. For more strongly coupled regimes, the thesis extended the same philosophy toward fluid--robot multiphysics. In this setting, the no-slip interaction between the robot and the surrounding fluid is treated as a coupled constraint, allowing the simulator to capture sustained undulatory swimming and aggressive transient maneuvers such as C-start escapes. The purpose of this modeling work is not only to simulate swimming, but also to obtain gradients that can support gait optimization, sim-to-real transfer, feedback control, and shape design.

Together, the reduced-order POSM model and the strongly coupled fluid--robot simulator represent two complementary levels of abstraction. The former is efficient enough for stiffness optimization and hardware-oriented design. The latter provides a higher-fidelity route toward modeling complex fluid--structure interaction. This combination reflects a broader design principle of the thesis: different models are useful at different stages of aquatic robot development, but they should remain connected through shared physical structure and experimental validation.

\section{Embodied Robotic Platforms}

The thesis also developed robotic platforms that connect modeling and optimization to real aquatic systems. These platforms are important because bio-inspired swimming cannot be evaluated fully in simulation alone. Fluid loading, fabrication tolerances, actuator limits, waterproofing constraints, and free-swimming body dynamics all influence the behavior of the final robot.

The POSM experiments used both fixed-base and untethered platforms. The fixed-base testbed isolated propulsive force generation, allowing controlled comparisons among optimized stiffness schedules, static stiffness baselines, and spatially uniform stiffness baselines. The untethered fish-inspired robot then tested whether these gains could transfer to free swimming. This progression from fixed-base force measurement to untethered validation provided a practical pathway for evaluating stiffness-modulated locomotion.

In addition, the thesis introduced SwimGym as an accessible platform for bio-inspired swimming research. SwimGym combines low-cost, modular hardware with a simulation environment, creating a foundation for sim-to-real studies of swimming robots. By using commercial off-the-shelf components and 3D-printed structures, the platform lowers the barrier to entry for experimental aquatic robotics while maintaining a connection to physics-based modeling. Together, these platforms support the broader goal of making bio-inspired swimming research more reproducible, extensible, and experimentally grounded.

\section{Sensing as a Future Direction for Closed-Loop Swimming}

Although sensing is not the main completed contribution of this thesis, it is an important direction for extending the presented work toward autonomous aquatic robots. Most experiments in this thesis focus on open-loop or externally measured performance, such as thrust, swimming speed, and trajectory tracking. However, robots operating outside controlled laboratory tanks will require onboard sensing to estimate their body state, detect changes in the surrounding flow, and adapt their locomotion in real time.

This thesis therefore treats sensing as an enabling layer for future closed-loop control rather than as a separate finished system. Several sensing directions naturally follow from the platforms developed here. Proprioceptive sensing can estimate joint motion, body shape, and stiffness state. Local hydrodynamic sensing, such as pressure-based flow measurements inspired by the fish lateral line, can provide information about inflow conditions, vortices, nearby boundaries, and disturbances. Onboard optical tracking or compact inertial sensing can further reduce dependence on external cameras and motion-capture systems. These capabilities would make it possible to close the loop between the robot's physical interaction with the fluid and its stiffness, gait, or control policy.

The continuum-robot sensing work presented in this thesis provides one example of this broader principle. Although that platform is not an aquatic swimmer, it demonstrates how modular mechanical design and embedded sensing can be combined to estimate robot configuration without relying entirely on external infrastructure. A similar philosophy can be applied to future aquatic platforms: distributed local sensing can be integrated with variable-stiffness mechanisms and differentiable fluid--robot models to enable feedback policies that respond to changing flow environments.

\section{Future Directions: Flow-Aware Control}

A promising future direction is to develop flow-aware control frameworks for bio-inspired swimmers operating in complex multiphysics environments. Rather than optimizing locomotion using the full simulated fluid--robot state, future work can investigate how a swimmer can infer and exploit the surrounding flow through onboard sensing, such as local pressure measurements. These partial observations are especially important for physical hardware, where full-field flow information is unavailable and locomotion emerges from coupled interactions among compliant body structures, actuation, sensing, and fluid dynamics.

By combining data-driven control with physics-based or differentiable fluid--robot simulation, it may be possible to train policies that are both physically grounded and robust to environmental disturbances, modeling mismatch, and unsteady flow structures. This would shift the role of simulation from gait design alone toward the co-design of sensing, control, and morphology, enabling robotic swimmers that can adaptively navigate and maneuver by feeling the fluid around them.

\section{Outlook}

Taken together, the results of this thesis show that programmable compliance, variational modeling, optimization-based propulsion, embodied platform design, and onboard sensing form a coherent design space for bio-inspired aquatic robots. The main lesson is that morphology, dynamics, and control should not be optimized independently. Instead, the physical body and the surrounding fluid should be treated as part of the computation that produces locomotion.

Several future directions follow directly from this work. First, POSM can be extended from open-loop optimized stiffness schedules to closed-loop stiffness control under unsteady inflow, obstacle interaction, and environmental disturbances. Second, strongly coupled fluid--robot multiphysics can be integrated more directly into stiffness optimization, allowing high-fidelity fluid interaction to inform mechanism design and control. Third, distributed sensing, including pressure-based flow sensing and proprioceptive body-state estimation, can be added to the swimming platforms to support autonomous adaptation outside laboratory conditions. Finally, the design principles explored in this thesis can be extended to other soft and bio-inspired robots, where morphology, compliance, sensing, and dynamics must be jointly engineered to achieve robust physical intelligence.
