\chapter{Introduction} \label{secIntro}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_motivation}

Aquatic environments cover the majority of the Earth's surface and play a central role in environmental monitoring, ecological assessment, infrastructure inspection, and resource exploration. Conventional underwater robots, such as propeller-driven autonomous underwater vehicles, have demonstrated reliable open-water operation, but their rigid morphology, limited maneuverability at low speeds, and disruptive wakes make them less suitable for confined, cluttered, or ecologically sensitive environments. In contrast, biological swimmers, from larval fish and aquatic insects to tuna, eels, and cephalopods, achieve a combination of efficiency, agility, and adaptability across a wide range of swimming regimes~\cite{breder1926locomotion,Fish2014,lucas_bending_2014,daniel_flexible_2002,dewey_scaling_2013,van2019scaling,zhu2019tuna,Prakash_2024,wang2020development}. These observations have motivated a growing interest in \emph{bio-inspired aquatic robots} that draw on the morphological and dynamical principles of natural swimmers.

A unifying feature of biological aquatic locomotion is that propulsion is not produced by an isolated actuator acting on a passive structure, but instead emerges from the strongly coupled interaction between body morphology, distributed compliance, neuromuscular actuation, sensory feedback, and the surrounding flow. Fast-swimming fish such as tuna and mackerel coordinate axial and caudal-peduncle musculature to modulate body stiffness during high-acceleration body--caudal-fin (BCF) undulation~\cite{cromie_lear_biomechanical_2020,boudrias_are_2002,hemmati_effects_2019,nedic_vortex_2015}, while bluegill sunfish actively tune the compliance of their pectoral fins between power and recovery strokes during drag-based paddling~\cite{floryan_distributed_2020,long_stiffness_1992}. Across these systems, mechanical properties such as joint stiffness, fin compliance, and body shape are not fixed design parameters; rather, they are time-varying control variables that shape the unsteady fluid--body interaction underlying thrust generation and maneuvering~\cite{domenici_kinematics_1997,john_h_long_importance_1996,lighthill_large-amplitude_1997}.

Realizing such behavior in robotic systems remains substantially more difficult than in nature. First, the hardware design space has typically been constrained by mechanisms that cannot simultaneously deliver time-varying, joint-specific, and easily integrable compliance~\cite{obayashi_online_2025,zhong_tunable_2021,liao_fast_2024,wang_design_2025,wolf_fish-like_2020,sharifzadeh_curvature-induced_2021,kwak_development_2023,Bobing_Octopus_2025,liu_tunable_2023,jusufi_undulatory_2017,HVS_2023}. Second, accurate prediction and design of swimming behavior require models that resolve the strongly coupled exchange of momentum at the fluid--body interface, but many robotics-oriented simulators couple rigid-body and fluid solvers only loosely, limiting their fidelity for sustained undulation, large-amplitude flapping, and transient maneuvers~\cite{angelidis2022gazebo,lee2023aquarium,genesis,lee2025unifiedframeworksimulatingstronglycoupled}. Third, deployment in realistic environments requires onboard sensing and inference that capture both the robot's internal configuration and its interaction with the flow, yet much of the prior literature still depends on external infrastructure such as motion-capture systems or fixed cameras for state estimation~\cite{sincak2024sensing,lu2023image,fang_design_2023,lilge_continuum_2022,stella_soft_2024,baaij_learning_2023}.

This thesis is motivated by the observation that these three challenges, embodied mechanism design, strongly coupled fluid--robot modeling and optimization, and integrated onboard sensing, cannot be addressed independently. They are different facets of the same underlying problem: how to build aquatic robots whose morphology, dynamics, and sensing are jointly designed and exploited within a single, principled framework.

\section{Thesis Statement and Approach}
\label{sec:intro_statement}

The central premise of this thesis is that bio-inspired aquatic locomotion is best understood and engineered as a problem of \emph{embodied multiphysics}: morphology, distributed compliance, actuation, sensing, and the surrounding fluid form a tightly coupled dynamical system, and progress requires methods that treat this coupling explicitly rather than as an afterthought. Building on this perspective, the thesis develops a coherent set of design, modeling, and sensing tools for bio-inspired aquatic robots, organized around three complementary directions.

First, on the \emph{mechanism} side, the thesis develops a programmable online stiffness modulation framework that enables time-varying, joint-specific compliance with simple actuation, allowing the same hardware to realize qualitatively different swimming gaits. Second, on the \emph{modeling and optimization} side, the thesis adopts variational mechanics as a unifying language: articulated multibody dynamics, quasi-steady hydrodynamic forces, and strongly coupled fluid--robot interaction are derived from a common least-action principle and discretized using structure-preserving variational integrators. The resulting models are stable, differentiable, and well-posed for trajectory optimization, gait design, and feedback control. Third, on the \emph{embodied sensing} side, the thesis develops integrated mechatronic platforms and modular sensing strategies that enable onboard estimation and feedback without reliance on external infrastructure, supporting both controlled experimental studies and more autonomous operation.

Across these directions, an emphasis is placed on consistency between simulation and hardware: differentiable physics models inform mechanism design and trajectory optimization, calibrated stiffness and hydrodynamic maps are used to translate optimized schedules into hardware commands, and untethered platforms are constructed so that optimized behaviors can be validated in real water rather than only in simulation.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The technical contributions of this thesis are organized into four chapters, each addressing a distinct aspect of embodied design, locomotion, and sensing for bio-inspired aquatic robots.

\paragraph{Programmable Online Stiffness Modulation for Optimized Aquatic Locomotion.}
Chapter~\ref{chapPOSM} introduces the programmable online stiffness modulation (POSM) framework, a modular multi-link arm in which sliding laminate flexures at each joint provide time-varying, joint-specific stiffness with simple two-channel actuation. The chapter develops a variational mechanics model of the mechanism with quasi-steady hydrodynamic forces~\cite{najafi_numerical_2017,izadi_rigid_2014}, formulates a trajectory-optimization problem that treats the within-cycle joint stiffness as a control variable, and uses the resulting schedules to inversely design flexure geometries that realize them in hardware. Experimental studies on a fixed-base testbed and on an untethered fish-like robot capable of both BCF and drag-based pectoral-fin swimming show that programmable joint-specific stiffness modulation improves thrust, swimming speed, and cost of transport across a broad frequency band when compared with static or spatially uniform baselines. These results constitute the variable-stiffness component of the thesis and connect prior biomechanical observations on muscle-mediated compliance~\cite{cromie_lear_biomechanical_2020,floryan_distributed_2020,long_stiffness_1992,lighthill_large-amplitude_1997} to a concrete, controllable hardware mechanism.

\paragraph{Mechatronics Design of Local-Sensing Swimming Robots.}
Chapter~\ref{chapdesign} addresses the embodied-hardware side of the thesis by developing two complementary aquatic-robot platforms. The first is a modular undulatory swimming robot equipped with multi-sensory feedback that supports both steady forward swimming and highly transient maneuvers. The second, SwimGym, is a low-cost, open-source drag-based swimming platform designed for reproducibility and sim-to-real studies; it consists of an untethered cephalopod-inspired robot, a portable vision-tracked testbed, and a strongly coupled fluid--robot simulation environment~\cite{lee2025unifiedframeworksimulatingstronglycoupled}. Together, these platforms provide the hardware substrate on which the modeling and control developments of later chapters are evaluated, and they situate this work within ongoing efforts to make bio-inspired swimming research more accessible to the broader robotics community~\cite{kim2024development, kwak2023development, kwak2017design, hu2024design, hartmann2025highly, wu2024multimodal, breder1926locomotion, Fish2014, van2019scaling, zhu2019tuna, Prakash_2024, wang2020development}.

\paragraph{Differentiable Fluid--Robot Simulation and Optimization-Based Control.}
Chapter~\ref{chapControl} develops the modeling and control core of the thesis. A unified variational formulation of articulated robot dynamics and incompressible Navier--Stokes flow is summarized, in which the no-slip condition at the fluid--robot interface is treated as an integral, weak-form constraint and the coupled action is discretized through variational time integration. The resulting simulator is implicit, differentiable, and stable for both sustained undulatory swimming and aggressive transient maneuvers such as C-start escapes; experimental comparisons on a multilink eel robot show substantial improvements over a loosely coupled smoothed-particle-hydrodynamics baseline~\cite{genesis,lee2025unifiedframeworksimulatingstronglycoupled,angelidis2022gazebo,lee2023aquarium}. Building on this foundation, the chapter develops three optimization- and control-oriented studies: an iterative learning control scheme for sim-to-real transfer of optimized gaits, an LQR-based feedback stabilization scheme for pose regulation under flow disturbances, and a shape-optimization study targeting a passively stable swimmer. These studies illustrate how differentiable, physically grounded simulators can support optimization-based design and control across multiple operating regimes.

\paragraph{In-Situ Self-Pose Estimation for Modular Continuum Robots.}
Chapter~\ref{chapLimbs} broadens the embodied-sensing component of the thesis from rigid articulated swimmers to compliant continuum bodies, where local sensing and modular reconfigurability are particularly impactful~\cite{russo2023continuum,yamauchi2022development,burgner2015continuum,wang2021design,abah2021multi}. The chapter introduces a modular continuum robotic platform built from interchangeable rigid segments connected by 3D-printed compliant joints with finite-element-characterized bending stiffness, enabling task-specific curvature distributions through modular assembly. A self-contained pose-estimation pipeline is then developed using electromagnetically actuated coils and onboard magnetometers, with a compact neural model mapping local magnetic measurements to inter-segment orientations. The system avoids external tracking during operation~\cite{sincak2024sensing,lu2023image,fang_design_2023,lilge_continuum_2022,stella_soft_2024,baaij_learning_2023} and is validated in traversal, contact-rich grasping, and large-deformation manipulation tasks. While the platform itself is continuum rather than aquatic, the underlying principles---modular design with programmable mechanical properties and embedded proprioception that scales across segments---directly inform the broader theme of distributed embodied sensing pursued throughout the thesis.

Collectively, these chapters contribute (i) a programmable variable-stiffness mechanism with associated dynamics modeling and gait optimization for bio-inspired swimming, (ii) untethered embodied platforms that support both fixed-base experiments and free-swimming validation, (iii) a strongly coupled, differentiable fluid--robot simulation framework with associated optimization- and control-based studies, and (iv) a modular continuum platform with embedded proprioceptive sensing that demonstrates how distributed local sensing can replace external tracking infrastructure.

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:intro_organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chapBackground} reviews the background most relevant to the contributions of this thesis, including bio-inspired aquatic locomotion modalities and the role of compliance, sensing and mechatronic design for swimming robots, and modeling and control of fluid--robot interaction. Chapter~\ref{chapPOSM} presents the programmable online stiffness modulation framework, including its mechanism design, variational dynamics model, stiffness optimization, and hardware validation in both fixed-base and untethered configurations. Chapter~\ref{chapdesign} describes the mechatronic design of local-sensing swimming robots, encompassing both the modular undulatory platform and the open-source SwimGym drag-based platform together with their experimental setups. Chapter~\ref{chapControl} develops the differentiable fluid--robot multiphysics simulator and its use for gait optimization, iterative learning control for sim-to-real transfer, LQR-based stabilization under flow disturbances, and shape optimization toward a passively stable swimmer. Chapter~\ref{chapLimbs} presents the in-situ self-pose estimation framework for modular continuum robots, including the modular joint design, the magnetic-sensing pipeline, and application demonstrations in traversal and grasping. Chapter~\ref{chapTEGs} examines printable thermoelectric ink as a candidate material technology for thermally responsive robotic devices, complementing the structural and sensing contributions developed in earlier chapters. Finally, Chapter~\ref{chapConclusions} summarizes the contributions of the thesis and discusses limitations and directions for future work.
