\chapter{Introduction} \label{secIntro}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_motivation}

Aquatic environments cover the majority of the Earth's surface and play a central role in environmental monitoring, ecological assessment, infrastructure inspection, and resource exploration. Conventional underwater robots, especially propeller-driven autonomous underwater vehicles, have enabled reliable operation in many open-water survey and inspection missions~\cite{yuh2000design}. However, for tasks that require close-proximity interaction with confined, cluttered, or ecologically sensitive environments, conventional rigid propulsive systems can be limited by their low-speed maneuverability, mechanical disturbance to the surrounding flow, and dependence on propulsor architectures that are not well matched to highly unsteady or cluttered settings. In contrast, biological swimmers, from larval fish and aquatic insects to tuna, eels, and cephalopods, achieve efficient, agile, and adaptive locomotion across a wide range of swimming regimes~\cite{sfakiotakis1999review,lauder2006learning,fish2013advantages,fish2006passive,li2022comprehensive,sun2022recent}. These observations have motivated growing interest in \emph{bio-inspired aquatic robots} that draw on the morphological and dynamical principles of natural swimmers rather than relying only on conventional propulsive architectures.

A unifying feature of biological aquatic locomotion is that propulsion is not produced by an isolated actuator acting on an otherwise passive structure. Instead, thrust generation and maneuvering emerge from the coupled interaction among body morphology, distributed compliance, actuation timing, and the surrounding flow. In undulatory swimmers, body stiffness and damping influence the traveling waveform, energetic cost of bending, thrust production, wake structure, and propulsive efficiency~\cite{long_stiffness_1992,john_h_long_importance_1996,tytell2010interactions,tytell2016role}. More generally, flexible fins and foils can substantially alter thrust and efficiency through the interaction between structural deformation and fluid loading~\cite{daniel_flexible_2002,dewey_scaling_2013,floryan_distributed_2020}. In pectoral-fin and flapping-fin propulsion, fin-ray stiffness and distributed flexibility similarly shape the hydrodynamic forces generated during each stroke~\cite{tangorra2010effect,aiello2018relationship,nguyen2017curvature}. Across these biological and robotic systems, mechanical properties such as body stiffness, joint compliance, and fin shape are therefore not merely fixed design parameters. They directly influence the phase, amplitude, and spatial distribution of fluid loading, and can become important variables for improving thrust, efficiency, and maneuverability.

Realizing these capabilities in robotic systems remains challenging. First, although prior work has shown that tunable stiffness, flexible-tail optimization, and stiffness-adjustable appendages can improve swimming speed, thrust, efficiency, or cost of transport in fish-like robots~\cite{jusufi_undulatory_2017,wolf_fish-like_2020,zhong_tunable_2021,quinn2022tunable,li2018soft,lu_development_2022,liu_tunable_2023,HVS_2023,kwak_development_2023,liao_fast_2024,obayashi_online_2025}, many existing mechanisms remain limited in simultaneously providing compact integration, joint-specific stiffness control, and within-cycle stiffness modulation on untethered platforms. As a result, stiffness is often treated as a manually selected mechanical parameter or a low-dimensional design choice, rather than as a time-varying control input that can be optimized over a swimming cycle.

Second, optimizing aquatic robots requires models that are accurate enough to capture fluid--robot interaction while also being structured enough for gradient-based design and control. Experimental and data-driven optimization methods have demonstrated that gait selection for fish robots is a complex multiphysical problem involving propulsion, power consumption, and fluid--structure interaction~\cite{abbaszadehExperimentalOptimizationFish2024,sharifzadehManeuverableGaitSelection2021,hu_design_2024}. However, direct experimental optimization can be costly, while high-fidelity fluid simulation can be computationally expensive or difficult to differentiate. This motivates reduced-order and structure-preserving models that can retain the dominant mechanics of swimming while remaining suitable for trajectory optimization, stiffness scheduling, and hardware design.

Third, the relevant dynamics are fundamentally coupled. Aquatic locomotion involves articulated multibody motion, elastic joint deformation, actuation constraints, hydrodynamic loading, and, in more strongly coupled regimes, the exchange of momentum across the fluid--robot interface. Variational mechanics and geometric numerical integration provide a principled foundation for deriving mechanical models and structure-preserving time discretizations~\cite{marsden2001discrete,hairer2006geometric}. Variational principles have also long been used in continuum mechanics and fluid dynamics~\cite{seligerVariationalPrinciplesContinuum1968a}, suggesting a natural route toward unified formulations of robot and fluid dynamics. Recent work in differentiable and robotics-oriented fluid--structure interaction has begun to make such models useful for simulation and optimization~\cite{lee2023aquarium,angelidisSmoothedParticleHydrodynamics2025,lee2026realizingroboticswimmingunified}. Nevertheless, reliable sim-to-real optimization for free-swimming, large-amplitude, bio-inspired aquatic robots remains difficult.

Finally, although flow sensing is not the central focus of this thesis, autonomous aquatic operation still requires the robot to estimate its state and, eventually, its interaction with the surrounding flow. Artificial lateral-line and pressure-sensing systems demonstrate the value of local hydrodynamic measurements for underwater robots~\cite{zhai2021lateral,ristroph2015lateral,xu2017pressure}. In this thesis, such sensing is treated primarily as an enabling layer for experimental validation, onboard estimation, and future closed-loop autonomy, while the main technical emphasis remains on variable-stiffness design, variational modeling, and optimization-based locomotion.

This thesis is motivated by the observation that these challenges are tightly connected. Variable-stiffness mechanisms create new control authority, but their benefit depends on models that can optimize stiffness schedules and predict propulsive forces. Variational and differentiable models enable such optimization, but they must be grounded in calibrated mechanisms and validated on physical swimming platforms. Sensing and feedback, even when treated as supporting components, are important for closing the loop between model predictions and real aquatic operation. The central problem addressed in this thesis is therefore how to design, model, optimize, and experimentally validate aquatic robots whose morphology, compliance, dynamics, and sensing are considered together rather than in isolation.

\section{Thesis Statement and Approach}
\label{sec:intro_statement}

The central premise of this thesis is that bio-inspired aquatic locomotion is best understood and engineered as a problem of \emph{embodied multiphysics}: the robot body, compliant structures, actuators, sensors, and surrounding fluid form a coupled dynamical system, and locomotion emerges from their interaction. This perspective is closely related to embodied intelligence and morphological computation, where behavior is shaped not only by a controller but also by the physical body and its environment~\cite{pfeifer2007self,pfeifer2009morphological}. In this thesis, that idea is developed in the specific context of aquatic locomotion, with particular emphasis on programmable stiffness, variational dynamics, and optimization-based propulsion.

The thesis follows three complementary directions. First, on the \emph{mechanism} side, it develops a programmable online stiffness modulation framework that enables time-varying, joint-specific compliance using a compact and mechanically simple architecture. Rather than treating stiffness as a fixed property selected before fabrication, the framework treats stiffness as a within-cycle control input that can be optimized to improve thrust generation and locomotion efficiency. This direction forms the main variable-stiffness contribution of the thesis.

Second, on the \emph{modeling and optimization} side, the thesis uses variational mechanics as a unifying language for aquatic robot dynamics. Articulated multibody dynamics, elastic joint behavior, actuation, and quasi-steady hydrodynamic loading are formulated in a way that supports trajectory optimization over both motion and stiffness schedules. For more strongly coupled settings, the same modeling philosophy is extended toward fluid--robot multiphysics, where the interaction between an articulated swimmer and incompressible flow is represented through coupled constraints and differentiable simulation. The goal is not only to simulate swimming, but to obtain models that provide useful gradients for gait optimization, stiffness scheduling, shape design, and feedback control.

Third, on the \emph{experimental platform and sensing} side, the thesis develops aquatic robotic platforms that allow the proposed mechanisms and models to be tested in real water. These systems include fixed-base testbeds for measuring thrust, untethered swimming robots for validating optimized gaits, and modular sensing components for estimating robot motion or local interaction with the environment. In this thesis, sensing is treated primarily as an enabling layer for experimental validation and future autonomy, while the main technical emphasis remains on variable-stiffness design, variational modeling, and optimization-based locomotion.

Across these directions, the thesis emphasizes consistency between model and hardware. Differentiable and variational models are used to guide mechanism design and trajectory optimization; calibrated stiffness and hydrodynamic maps translate optimized schedules into hardware commands; and physical robotic platforms are used to evaluate whether the optimized behaviors remain effective outside simulation.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The core technical contributions of this thesis are organized around embodied design, variational modeling, optimization-based propulsion, and experimental validation for bio-inspired aquatic robots.

\paragraph{Programmable Online Stiffness Modulation for Optimized Aquatic Locomotion.}
Chapter~\ref{chapPOSM} introduces the programmable online stiffness modulation (POSM) framework, a modular multi-link swimming appendage in which sliding laminate flexures at each joint provide time-varying, joint-specific stiffness with simple actuation. The chapter develops a variational mechanics model of the appendage with quasi-steady hydrodynamic forces~\cite{najafi_numerical_2017,izadi_rigid_2014}, and formulates trajectory-optimization problems in which joint stiffness schedules are optimized together with the swimming cycle. In this formulation, stiffness is treated as an explicit control variable for maximizing propulsive performance, rather than as a static material or geometric parameter.

The optimized stiffness schedules are then used to guide the inverse design of flexure geometries and the hardware commands required to realize them experimentally. This connects prior studies showing that body and tail stiffness strongly influence swimming performance~\cite{john_h_long_importance_1996,tytell2016role,zhong_tunable_2021,quinn2022tunable} with a robotic mechanism capable of joint-specific, time-varying stiffness control. Experimental studies are performed on both fixed-base and untethered robotic platforms. The fixed-base experiments isolate thrust generation and allow direct comparison between optimized time-varying stiffness, static stiffness, and spatially uniform baselines. The untethered experiments evaluate whether the same stiffness-modulation principles improve free-swimming performance in both body--caudal-fin and drag-based pectoral-fin modes. Together, these studies demonstrate how biomechanical observations about body and fin compliance can be translated into a controllable hardware mechanism and an optimization pipeline for aquatic propulsion.

\paragraph{Mechatronics Design of Experimental Aquatic Robot Platforms.}
Chapter~\ref{chapdesign} develops the robotic hardware needed to evaluate the modeling and locomotion methods of the thesis. The first platform is a modular undulatory swimming robot designed for experiments involving steady forward swimming, transient maneuvers, and model-based control. The second platform, SwimGym, is a low-cost and reproducible drag-based swimming system consisting of an untethered cephalopod-inspired robot, a portable experimental testbed, and a simulation environment. These platforms provide the physical substrate for validating optimized gaits, comparing simulation and hardware, and studying how body morphology and actuation interact with the surrounding fluid.

The emphasis of this chapter is on mechatronic integration rather than on sensing as a standalone contribution. Onboard electronics, modular actuation, tracking interfaces, and local sensing capabilities are developed to support repeatable experiments and future closed-loop studies. In this way, the chapter connects the mechanism and modeling contributions of the thesis to practical robotic systems that can operate in water, rather than only in numerical simulation or benchtop tests.

\paragraph{Variational Fluid--Robot Modeling and Optimization-Based Control.}
Chapter~\ref{chapControl} develops the modeling and control core of the thesis. It summarizes a unified variational formulation of articulated robot dynamics and incompressible fluid flow, in which the no-slip condition at the fluid--robot interface is treated as a coupled constraint and the resulting equations are discretized through structure-preserving variational time integration~\cite{seligerVariationalPrinciplesContinuum1968a,marsden2001discrete,hairer2006geometric,lee2023aquarium,lee2026realizingroboticswimmingunified}. This formulation is used to model sustained undulatory swimming and transient maneuvers such as C-start escapes, while preserving differentiability for optimization and control.

Building on this simulator, the chapter develops several optimization- and control-oriented studies. These include iterative learning control for transferring optimized gaits from simulation to hardware, feedback stabilization for pose regulation under flow disturbances, and shape optimization for passively stable swimming behavior. Compared with the variable-stiffness chapter, this chapter places less emphasis on new mechanism design and more emphasis on how variational and differentiable models can support optimization across robot motion, body shape, and control policy. The result is a modeling framework that complements the POSM work by extending optimization-based aquatic locomotion from reduced-order hydrodynamic models toward more strongly coupled fluid--robot interaction. Related FSI and robotics-simulation approaches based on immersed-boundary, vortex-particle, SPH, and adjoint formulations provide important context for this direction~\cite{battistaIB2dPythonMATLAB2017a,bernierSimulationsPropellingEnergy2019a,bazilevsAdjointbasedControlFluidStructure2013,arranzFluidStructureInteraction2022,angelidisSmoothedParticleHydrodynamics2025}.

\paragraph{In-Situ Self-Pose Estimation for Modular Continuum Robots.}
Chapter~\ref{chapLimbs} broadens the embodied-sensing component of the thesis from articulated swimmers to modular continuum robots, where local proprioception and reconfigurable compliance are essential for operation without external tracking. The chapter introduces a modular continuum robotic platform built from interchangeable rigid segments connected by 3D-printed compliant joints with finite-element-characterized bending stiffness. A self-contained pose-estimation pipeline is then developed using electromagnetically actuated coils and onboard magnetometers, with a compact neural model mapping local magnetic measurements to inter-segment orientations.

Although this platform is not itself an aquatic swimmer, it addresses a related embodied-robotics problem: how to combine programmable mechanical properties, modular body design, and local sensing so that a robot can estimate and exploit its own configuration during contact-rich motion. The chapter therefore supports the broader theme of the thesis by showing how distributed mechanical design and embedded sensing can reduce dependence on external tracking infrastructure, while leaving detailed hydrodynamic sensing and flow-feedback control as future extensions.

Collectively, these chapters contribute: (i) a programmable variable-stiffness mechanism for bio-inspired swimming; (ii) variational dynamics and trajectory optimization methods that treat stiffness as a control variable for thrust generation; (iii) experimental aquatic platforms for fixed-base and untethered validation; (iv) a differentiable, strongly coupled fluid--robot simulation framework for optimization-based control and design; and (v) modular sensing strategies that support embodied estimation without relying entirely on external infrastructure.

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:intro_organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chapPOSM} presents the programmable online stiffness modulation framework, including its mechanism design, variational dynamics model, stiffness optimization, flexure inverse design, and hardware validation in both fixed-base and untethered configurations. Chapter~\ref{chapdesign} describes the mechatronic design of experimental aquatic robot platforms, including the modular undulatory swimmer and the SwimGym drag-based swimming system. Chapter~\ref{chapControl} develops the differentiable fluid--robot multiphysics simulator and its use for gait optimization, iterative learning control for sim-to-real transfer, feedback stabilization under flow disturbances, and shape optimization toward passively stable swimming. Chapter~\ref{chapLimbs} presents the in-situ self-pose estimation framework for modular continuum robots, including the modular joint design, magnetic-sensing pipeline, and application demonstrations in traversal and grasping. Chapter~\ref{chapTEGs} examines printable thermoelectric ink as a candidate material technology for thermally responsive robotic devices, complementing the structural and sensing contributions developed in earlier chapters. Finally, Chapter~\ref{chapConclusions} summarizes the contributions of the thesis and discusses limitations and directions for future work.
