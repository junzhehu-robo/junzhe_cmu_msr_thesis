\chapter{Mechatronics Design of Local-Sensing Swimming Robots} \label{chapdesign}

\section{Modular Undulatory Swimming Robot with Multi-sensory Feedback}
\label{sec:eel_robot}

The first platform developed in this thesis is a modular undulatory swimming robot intended to support both steady forward swimming and highly transient maneuvers such as C-start escape turns. The robot is realized as a five-link planar eel-like body whose link length, joint compliance, and onboard sensing can be reconfigured for different experimental needs. This modular mechatronic architecture serves a dual purpose: it provides a reusable hardware substrate for gait optimization and sim-to-real studies, and it acts as the physical reference against which the strongly coupled fluid--robot multiphysics simulator developed in Chapter~\ref{chapControl} is validated. In what follows, we describe the mechanical structure, electronics, experimental setup, and the two representative swimming regimes used throughout the thesis.

\subsection{Modular Structure Design}

The robot consists of five rigid planar links connected by four actuated revolute joints, with the head link instrumented for vision-based pose tracking. Each link is 3D-printed from a stiff polymer to keep the body lightweight while preserving rigid-body behavior under hydrodynamic loading, and adjacent links are joined through a modular interface that fixes a target link length while allowing the joint to be quickly replaced. This modularity allows the body kinematics to be reconfigured without rebuilding the platform: link length and joint stiffness can be varied to study how morphology shapes the traveling-wave gait and the trailing-edge amplitude during BCF undulation, and the same chassis supports the C-start configuration used for transient maneuvers.

The hull is sealed for surface swimming, with the electronics housed in a watertight cavity in the head link and the actuation wiring routed along the body. This combination of modular links and a self-contained head module enables the same robot to be deployed for repeated trials with minimal manual intervention between runs.

\subsection{Electronics Design}

Each joint is driven by a Dynamixel XC330 servomotor that accepts position commands generated from a parameterized gait. The four servos are coordinated by an OpenRB-150 control board housed in the head link, which exposes a USB and wireless interface for sending high-level gait parameters such as frequency, amplitude, and phase offset. The board also collects feedback from the integrated servo encoders and from onboard inertial sensing, providing multi-sensory feedback that supports both offline gait tuning and future closed-loop control studies.

Power is supplied by an onboard lithium battery sized for the duration of a swimming trial, and a regulated rail isolates the actuation bus from the microcontroller and sensing electronics to limit electrical noise during high-current servo transients. The control loop runs at a rate sufficient to track the prescribed joint trajectories during both steady undulation and the rapid joint excursions characteristic of C-start maneuvers.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The robot swims at the free surface of a still-water pool, which keeps the platform within the field of view of an overhead camera and simplifies wireless communication. Each link carries a unique AprilTag fiducial marker, allowing the camera system to recover the per-link position and heading throughout a trial. The vision pipeline records frames at a fixed sampling rate and post-processes the time series using cubic-spline interpolation to obtain smooth position and heading estimates for each link, which are then used both for performance evaluation and for sim-to-real comparison.

For each trial, gait parameters are uploaded to the robot, a calibration image of the AprilTags is recorded against a known reference, and the swimming sequence is executed in open loop. The fluid is allowed to settle between trials so that residual flow from previous strokes does not influence subsequent measurements. This setup enables repeatable measurements across amplitude sweeps for forward swimming as well as well-controlled initial conditions for C-start maneuvers.

\subsection{Forward Swimming}

Steady forward swimming is generated by a sinusoidal traveling-wave joint pattern in which each joint follows a sinusoid at a common frequency with a fixed phase offset along the body. The amplitude of the traveling wave is varied to span a representative range of BCF-style gaits, and twenty independent trials are recorded per amplitude to capture run-to-run variability.

For each gait, the measured head-link position and heading are extracted from the AprilTag time series and compared against the prediction of the differentiable fluid--robot simulator developed in Chapter~\ref{chapControl}. This comparison provides a direct test of how well a strongly coupled simulator can reproduce sustained undulatory swimming on real hardware, and forms the experimental anchor for the forward-swimming validation reported in that chapter.

\subsection{C-Start Agile Escape Maneuver}

The same platform is used to study the C-start escape, a rapid transient maneuver in which the body first bends into a C shape and then straightens to achieve a large heading change in a short time. Because the C-start involves highly unsteady vortex shedding and strong fluid--body interaction, it serves as a stringent test of the differentiable simulator's sim-to-real fidelity in the transient regime.

The C-start gait is parameterized by the joint-angle profiles over a fixed time horizon, allowing both an initial hand-designed guess and an optimized profile---computed via gradients from the differentiable simulator---to be uploaded to the same hardware. The robot is then released from a quiescent initial configuration, and the resulting heading-change trajectories are extracted from the AprilTag data. This use of the platform tightly couples mechatronic design, modeling, and optimization: the same modular robot is the substrate on which prescribed gaits, simulator-derived predictions, and simulator-optimized maneuvers are all evaluated against ground-truth pool experiments.







\section{A Robotics Platform for Drag Swimming}

Bioinspired swimming has gained considerable attention for efficient propulsion and maneuverability. However, complex system designs, unique actuation mechanisms, and modeling challenges have created barriers to entry for the broader robotics community. To address these issues, we introduce SwimGym: a low-cost platform for bioinspired swimming research. SwimGym comprises an untethered swimming robot, a portable hardware testbed, and a physics simulator based on the immersed boundary method. The robot design utilizes low-cost components and 3D printing to achieve a total cost less than \$200 USD. We demonstrate the robot's ability to achieve forward swimming with good sim-to-real agreement. The results highlight SwimGym as an effort towards a low-cost, open-source platform for bio-inspired swimming research that fully supports sim-to-real studies.

For example, the efficient, stable swimming of cephalopods and aquatic insects inspired various drag-based~\cite{kim2024development, kwak2023development, kwak2017design, hu2024design} mechanisms for robotic swimming. However, the diversity in actuator-mechanism design, specialized fabrication needs, and modeling difficulties pose significant challenges for reproducing these systems, which span different propulsion modes~\cite{hartmann2025highly, wu2024multimodal}. Furthermore, bio-inspired swimming involves complex fluid-robot interactions that are difficult to model for sim-to-real studies commonly found in robotics~\cite{angelidis2022gazebo, lee2023aquarium}. Therefore, we propose \textbf{SwimGym}: a low-cost, open-source robot platform specifically designed for bio-inspired swimming research as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:swimgym_overview}. SwimGym comprises an untethered swimming robot, a portable hardware testbed, and a corresponding simulation environment as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:swimgym_overview}. Utilizing COTS (commercial-off-the-shelf) components and 3D-printed parts, we design the robot from scratch to be modular and amenable to fabrication in house for under \$200 USD. In addition, a portable and reproducible testbed is provided for convenient testing of the robot in a fluid-filled environment with vision-based tracking. Finally, we utilize a unified-multiphysics approach to create a physics-based simulator of the test environment~\cite{lee2025unifiedframeworksimulatingstronglycoupled}. To showcase SwimGym's viability for swimming locomotion research, we demonstrate our robot achieving forward swimming in both simulation and on hardware. In future work, we plan to open-source the hardware design, experimental setup, and simulation code for use in downstream learning and controls tasks. In summary, SwimGym offers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An untethered, insect-inspired swimming robot with a low build cost and entry-level fabrication process.
    \item A portable, low-cost experimental testbed for conducting real-world swimming locomotion.
    \item A physics-based, digital-twin simulator of the test environment for use in sim-to-real studies.
\end{itemize}
 
 
\section{Drag Based Swimming Platform}
 
\subsection{Robot Hardware and Experimental Testbed}
 
Inspired by aquatic insects and turtles---whose propulsion rely on drag forces generated by rowing strokes---the SwimGym robot features a main body with servo-actuated fins and rudders as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware}. A gear assembly drives the fins symmetrically, while two miniature servos independently control each rudder via five-bar linkages for steering. The hardware assembly utilizes 3d-printed parts and commercially-available off-the-shelf (COTS) components as shown in Table~\ref{tab:parts_list}. The result is a low-cost (\$185) bill of material with an accessible fabrication process. An onboard, 7.4V battery powers the servos and onboard Adafruit Feather~M0 board, which integrates an ARM Cortex-M0 microcontroller with a low-power, long-range radio (LoRa) transceiver. This allows for truly untethered operation of the SwimGym robot while maintaining a stable, long-range wireless communication between the robot and a host computer.
 
To provide a portable swimming environment, we develop a low-cost experimental testbed consisting of a $122 \times 122 \times 40$\,cm pool and a vision-based tracking system as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:swimgym_overview}. The vision-based system requires only a (cellphone) camera and computer, which can also send motor commands wirelessly to the robot via LoRa.

\subsection{Physics Simulator}
 
To facilitate sim-to-real studies, we develop a simulated gym environment replicating our testbed. To do so, we leverage variational mechanics to develop a Navier-Stokes-based fluid-structure interaction solver for robotics~\cite{lee2025unifiedframeworksimulatingstronglycoupled}. We reduce the sim-to-real gap by incorporating CAD-calculated mass properties into the simulation model.
 
To validate our simulator, we perform a sim-to-real demonstration of a forward-swimming gait. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:simtoreal}, SwimGym's simulator is able to properly simulate the forward-swimming behavior consistent with the demonstration on hardware. Meanwhile, Genesis~\cite{genesis}, a state-of-the-art world simulator for robotics, is unable to simulate the achieved forward motion. Specifically, our framework achieves an RMSE error of 0.89~cm over the whole trajectory, a 90\% improvement over Genesis (8.86~cm RMSE error).
